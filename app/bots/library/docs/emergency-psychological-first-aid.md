# Psychological first aid, children and pets (emergency notes)

## Psychological first aid (look, listen, link)

Psychological first aid is calm, practical support for people who have just
been through something frightening. It is not therapy and anyone can give
it. Look: check for safety, urgent needs and people in serious distress.
Listen: approach gently, introduce yourself, ask about needs and concerns,
listen without pushing, and help them feel calm. Link: help with basic needs
(water, food, shelter, information), help them reach family and services,
and help them cope with the next steps.

## Helping someone who is panicking or in shock

Stay calm and speak slowly in a quiet voice. Introduce yourself and say you
are there to help. Move them away from the scene if it is safe. Help them
slow their breathing: breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4, hold
for 4, and repeat with them. Ground them in the present: name 5 things they
can see, 4 they can touch, 3 they can hear. Offer water and a blanket, and do
not leave them alone.

## What to say and what not to say

Do: listen more than you talk, accept their feelings, tell them their
reactions are normal after something abnormal, give clear and true
information, and respect their choices. Do not: force them to talk about
what happened, tell them how they should feel, say "it could be worse" or
"everything happens for a reason", make promises you cannot keep, or share
their story with others.

## If you want to kill yourself (crisis lines, suicide hotline)

If you feel suicidal, are thinking about suicide or want to kill yourself, you are not alone
and you deserve help right now. Tell someone near you, or someone on the mesh,
how you feel and ask them to stay with you. Call or text a crisis line: 988 in
the US and Canada, 116 123 (Samaritans) in the UK and Ireland, or your local
emergency number (911, 112, 999, 000). Move away from anything you could use
to hurt yourself. These feelings can pass with support, even when it does not
feel that way.

## Grief and loss after a disaster

Shock, numbness, anger, guilt, sleeplessness and crying are all normal. Help
with practical things, encourage them to keep routines, eat, sleep and stay
with people they trust, and to avoid alcohol and drugs. Most people recover
with time and support. Help them find professional support if, after a few
weeks, they cannot function, or if they talk about harming themselves.

## Someone who is suicidal (talking about suicide)

If a friend, a relative or anyone else seems suicidal, talks about suicide
or about wanting to kill himself or herself, take it seriously and ask
directly: "Are you thinking about killing
yourself?" Asking does not put the idea in their head. Stay with them, remove
access to weapons, medicines and other means if you can safely, listen
without judging, and get help: emergency services, or a crisis line (988 in
the US and Canada by call or text, 116 123 Samaritans in the UK and Ireland).

## Helping children and kids through a disaster

Keep children with their family or caregivers whenever possible, and keep
routines (meals, bedtime, small chores). Tell them the truth simply, in
words they understand, and that adults are working to keep them safe. Limit
what they see and hear of the news. Let them ask questions, draw and play
about it: that is how children process events. Regression (bedwetting,
clinginess, tantrums) is normal for a while. Look after yourself: children
read your calm.

## Children separated from their family

Stay with a lost child, do not take them away from the area, and tell
emergency workers or the people running the shelter. Write down where and
when you found them, what they are wearing and anything they say about their
family. Give children in an emergency a card with their name, parents' names
and phone numbers to carry.

## Pets in an emergency or evacuation

Take pets with you when you evacuate: if it is not safe for you, it is not
safe for them. Pack food and water for at least 3 days, bowls, leash or
carrier, medicines, vaccination records, a recent photo, and litter or bags.
Make sure they wear ID tags or are microchipped with up-to-date details. Find
out in advance which shelters, hotels or friends will take pets.

## Pets after a disaster

Animals can be scared and behave unpredictably: keep them leashed or in a
carrier, and keep them away from debris, floodwater and downed lines. Keep
them indoors during extreme heat or cold. Watch for injuries and stress
(hiding, not eating, aggression). A lost pet is most often found nearby:
search the area, put up notices, and check shelters.
