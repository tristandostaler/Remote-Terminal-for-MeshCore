# Navigation and survival outdoors (emergency notes)

## Survival priorities (rule of threes)

A person can survive about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in
harsh weather, 3 days without water, and 3 weeks without food. So the order
is: immediate danger and first aid, then shelter and warmth, then water, then
signalling for rescue, then food.

## Lost (STOP)

Stop: stay where you are and calm down. Think: how did you get here, who
knows where you are. Observe: landmarks, weather, supplies, daylight left.
Plan: if someone knows your route, staying put makes you easier to find.
Otherwise move toward a known road, trail or river, marking your way.

## Find north without a compass (sun and shadow)

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. At midday
(around 1 pm with daylight saving time) the sun is due south in the northern
hemisphere and due north in the southern hemisphere, outside the tropics.
Shadow stick method: push a stick upright into flat ground, mark the tip of
its shadow, wait 15 to 30 minutes and mark the new tip. The line from the
first mark to the second points roughly west to east.

## Find north at night (North Star, Southern Cross)

Northern hemisphere: find the Big Dipper; the two stars at the outer edge of
its bowl point to Polaris, the North Star, about five times their distance
away. Polaris is almost exactly north, and its height above the horizon is
roughly your latitude. Southern hemisphere: extend the long axis of the
Southern Cross about four and a half times its length; the point below that
on the horizon is roughly south.

## Daylight left before sunset

Hold your arm straight out, hand flat and sideways, fingers together, with
your little finger resting on the horizon. Count the finger widths between
the horizon and the bottom of the sun: each finger is about 15 minutes, a
hand of four fingers about an hour. Rough, and less accurate far from the
equator.

## Estimating distance

Counting paces: an adult pace (two steps) is about 1.5 m, so about 65 paces
per 100 m; count your own on a known distance, and expect more paces uphill
and in rough terrain. Walking pace on flat trail is about 4 to 5 km per hour.
Thunder: seconds between flash and thunder divided by 3 gives kilometres.

## Map coordinates and sharing your position

Give GPS positions as decimal latitude, longitude, e.g. 45.5017, -73.5673
(north and east positive, south and west negative). Phones show your GPS
position even with no signal or internet: in a maps or compass app, or by
long-pressing your location. Read the digits twice and send them in writing
when you can, rather than by voice.

## Emergency shelter

Get out of wind, rain and cold first, and insulate from the ground: the
ground steals heat faster than air. Use a tent, tarp, vehicle, or a lean-to or
debris hut from branches and thick layers of leaves. Keep it small so your
body heat warms it. Avoid valleys where cold air pools, dry riverbeds, and
spots under dead trees.

## Starting a fire

A fire needs heat, fuel and air. Gather everything before lighting: tinder
(dry grass, birch bark, cotton balls with petroleum jelly, lint), kindling
(dry twigs from pencil-lead to thumb thickness, snapped from standing dead
branches) and fuel wood (wrist thick and bigger). Build a small teepee of
kindling over the tinder, light it from the upwind side, add bigger wood
gradually. Clear the ground, keep it away from overhanging branches, and put
it out completely with water and stirring.

## Staying warm and dry

Cotton loses its warmth when wet: wool and synthetics keep insulating. Dress
in layers and remove one before you sweat. Cover the head, neck and hands.
Eat and drink: the body needs fuel to make heat. Change out of wet clothes and
share body heat in a sleeping bag if someone is getting too cold.

## Finding water outdoors

Look downhill, in valleys, where plants are green, and follow animal tracks
and birds at dawn and dusk. Collect rain with a tarp, and dew by wiping grass
with a cloth in the morning. Melt snow before drinking rather than eating it.
Always purify water from streams and lakes (boil it, or use chlorine or a
filter); see the water notes.

## Wild food safety

Do not eat wild plants or mushrooms you cannot identify with certainty: many
are poisonous and some are deadly, and the "universal edibility test" is not
reliable. Food matters much less than water and warmth in the first days.
Cook fish, meat and shellfish thoroughly.

## Signalling for rescue outdoors

Stay visible in the open near your shelter. Use a whistle (three blasts), a
mirror or phone screen to flash sunlight, bright clothing spread out,
ground-to-air symbols (see the radio notes), and a smoky fire by day (add green
leaves) or a bright one at night. Turn your phone on at set times to save
battery; a text may get out where a call cannot.
