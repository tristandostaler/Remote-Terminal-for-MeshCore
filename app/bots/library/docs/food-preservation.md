# Food preservation and storage

## Freezing food

Freeze at -18 °C (0 °F) or colder. Cool cooked food quickly and freeze in
flat, airtight bags with the air pressed out, labelled with the date.
Blanch most vegetables first (boil 1 to 3 minutes, then plunge into ice
water) to keep colour and texture. Rough freezer times for best quality:
ground meat 3 to 4 months, steaks and roasts 6 to 12 months, poultry 9 to 12
months, cooked dishes 2 to 3 months, bread 3 months, most vegetables 8 to 12
months. Thaw in the fridge or cold water, not on the counter.

## Drying and dehydrating food

Dry fruit and vegetables at about 57 to 63 °C (135 to 145 °F) in a
dehydrator or an oven with the door slightly open, in thin even slices, until
leathery (fruit) or brittle (vegetables). Dip apples and pears in lemon water
to stop browning. Jerky: use lean meat, slice thin, and heat it to 71 °C
(160 °F) before or after drying to kill bacteria. Store dried food airtight in
a cool dark place; check for moisture or mould in the first week.

## Fermenting vegetables (sauerkraut, pickles)

Lacto-fermentation needs only salt and vegetables. Sauerkraut: shred cabbage,
mix with 2 percent salt by weight (20 g salt per kg of cabbage), massage
until it releases liquid, pack tightly into a jar so the brine covers it, and
keep it under the brine with a weight. Ferment at 18 to 22 °C for 1 to 4
weeks, then refrigerate. For whole vegetables (cucumbers, carrots) use a
brine of 2 to 5 percent salt (20 to 50 g per litre of water). A thin white
film (kahm yeast) can be skimmed off; fuzzy, coloured mould, a slimy texture
or a rotten smell means throw it out.

## Home canning safety (botulism)

Botulism toxin has no smell or taste and can kill. High-acid foods (most
fruit, jams, pickles, and tomatoes with added lemon juice or citric acid) can
be canned in a boiling water bath. Low-acid foods (vegetables, beans, meat,
fish, soups) must be pressure-canned at the time and pressure of a tested
recipe; a water bath is never enough for them. Use only tested recipes
(USDA, Ball or Bernardin), new lids, and adjust for altitude. Never can in an
oven or by just pouring hot food into jars. Throw away any jar with a bulging
or unsealed lid, leaking, spurting, mould or an off smell, without tasting it.

## How to store potatoes and vegetables without a fridge (root cellar)

Most root vegetables keep for months cold and humid: potatoes, carrots,
beets, turnips and cabbage at about 0 to 4 °C (potatoes 4 to 10 °C) with high
humidity, in the dark (light turns potatoes green and toxic; cut green parts
away). Onions, garlic and winter squash like it dry: onions and garlic cool,
squash and pumpkins at 10 to 15 °C. Keep apples away from other produce: they
give off ethylene gas that ripens and sprouts it. Store only undamaged
produce, and check often for rot.

## Storing eggs without refrigeration

Unwashed eggs with their natural coating keep about 2 to 3 weeks at cool room
temperature, longer if cool. Eggs sold washed (as in Canada and the US) have
lost that coating and should stay refrigerated. Float test: an egg that sinks
and lies flat is fresh; one that stands up is older but usually fine; one
that floats is old and should be thrown away. When in doubt, crack it into a
separate bowl and smell it.

## Shelf life of pantry staples (how long rice, flour and food keep)

Sealed and stored cool, dry and dark: white rice, dried pasta, sugar, salt
and honey keep for years (white rice 25 years or more in sealed oxygen-free
containers); dried beans keep for years but get harder to cook with age;
white flour about 1 year, whole-wheat flour 3 to 6 months (it goes rancid);
rolled oats 1 to 2 years; brown rice about 6 months (oils go rancid); canned
food 2 to 5 years, high-acid cans (tomato, fruit) 12 to 18 months for best
quality; cooking oil about 1 year once opened. Keep food in sealed containers
against insects and rodents.

## Making butter, yogurt and cheese without a store

Butter: shake or whip cold heavy cream until it separates into butter and
buttermilk, then rinse the butter in cold water until the water runs clear.
Yogurt: heat milk to 85 °C (185 °F), cool to about 43 °C (110 °F), stir in 2
tablespoons of plain yogurt with live cultures per litre, and keep warm for 6
to 12 hours. Fresh cheese (paneer, ricotta-style): bring milk nearly to a
boil, stir in lemon juice or vinegar until it curdles, and strain through a
cloth.

## Salting and curing meat

Salt draws water out of meat and slows bacteria, but cured sausages and hams
need curing salt (with nitrite) at exact amounts, controlled temperature and
humidity to be safe from botulism: follow a tested recipe and weigh
everything. Simple salt-packed fish or meat should still be cooked well
before eating.
