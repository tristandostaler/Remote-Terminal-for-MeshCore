# Radio and emergency communication (emergency notes)

## Emergency phone numbers

911 in the US and Canada; 112 in Europe and from most mobile phones worldwide
(it is redirected to the local number); 999 in the UK; 000 in Australia; 111
in New Zealand. A mobile phone can often reach emergency services through any
carrier's network, even without service from your own. Text-to-911 exists in
some areas: call if you can, text if you cannot speak. When networks are
overloaded, text messages often get through when calls do not.

## Sending an emergency message on the mesh

Keep it short and complete. Say who you are, where you are (address or GPS
coordinates, e.g. 45.5017, -73.5673), what happened, how many people, how
badly hurt, what you need, and when you sent it. Example: "EMERGENCY Jean at
12 Rue Main, 2 injured, 1 not breathing, need medic and car, 14:05". Send to
a channel people watch, repeat it every few minutes, and ask those who hear
it to acknowledge and relay. Save battery between messages.

## Mayday, Pan-Pan and Securite

Mayday: life-threatening emergency. Say "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY, this is
(name or call sign, three times), MAYDAY (name), my position is ..., nature
of distress ..., assistance required ..., number of people ..., other
information ..., OVER." Pan-Pan (said three times): urgent but not
immediately life-threatening, such as an injury needing help or a breakdown.
Securite (said three times): a safety warning to others, such as a hazard or
weather. Then listen: stop transmitting when someone answers.

## Marine VHF emergency channel

Channel 16 (156.800 MHz) is the international maritime distress, safety and
calling channel, monitored by coast guards and ships. Call there, then move
to a working channel if asked. Channel 9 (156.450 MHz) is a secondary calling
channel in the US. Many marine radios have a red DSC distress button that
sends your position automatically if a GPS is connected.

## Aviation emergency frequency

121.500 MHz is the international aviation emergency frequency (guard),
monitored by air traffic control and many aircraft; 243.000 MHz is the
military equivalent. Emergency locator beacons on aircraft also transmit
there. A personal locator beacon (PLB) uses 406 MHz satellites and reaches
rescue services anywhere, with no licence needed to activate it in an
emergency.

## CB radio emergency channels

CB (citizens band, 27 MHz, no licence needed in North America): channel 9
(27.065 MHz) is the emergency and traveller-assistance channel. Channel 19
(27.185 MHz) is used by truckers on highways and is a good place to ask for
road information or relay a call for help.

## FRS, GMRS and PMR446 walkie-talkie channels

FRS radios (no licence) share 22 channels with GMRS (licence needed in the US,
not in Canada) around 462 and 467 MHz. There is no official emergency
channel; community plans commonly use channel 1 or channel 3 (with no
privacy code), and some use GMRS channel 20 (462.675 MHz). In Europe, PMR446
radios have 16 channels near 446 MHz; channel 1 (446.00625 MHz) and channel 8
(446.09375 MHz) are common calling channels. Agree channels with your group
in advance. Range is typically 1 to 3 km, more from high ground.

## Amateur (ham) radio calling frequencies

The main 2 m FM simplex calling frequency in North America is 146.520 MHz;
on 70 cm it is 446.000 MHz. Local repeaters and emergency nets (ARES, RACES,
ARRL in the US, and similar groups elsewhere) are often active during
disasters. A licence is required to transmit, but the rules in the US and
many other countries allow anyone to use any means of radio communication to
call for help when there is an immediate threat to life and no other way to
reach help.

## Weather and emergency broadcast radio

In the US and Canada, NOAA Weather Radio and Environment Canada Weatheradio
broadcast weather warnings around the clock on these frequencies: 162.400, 162.425, 162.450, 162.475, 162.500,
162.525 and 162.550 MHz. Local AM and FM stations carry emergency alerts and
instructions; AM travels farther, especially at night. A battery or
hand-crank radio receives both.

## Distress signals without a radio (SOS, three of anything)

SOS in Morse code: three short, three long, three short (... --- ...), by
flashlight, whistle, horn or tapping. Three of anything signals distress:
three whistle blasts, three fires in a triangle, three shots, three flashes;
repeat at intervals. A whistle carries much farther than a voice and costs
less energy. At night, a flashlight pointed at an aircraft or ship; by day, a
mirror flashing sunlight toward it.

## Ground-to-air signals

Make symbols as large as possible, at least 3 m (10 feet) long, in a colour
that contrasts with the ground (rocks, branches, clothing, trampled snow). V
means we require assistance. X means we require medical assistance. N means
no, Y means yes. An arrow means we are proceeding in this direction. Lying
flat with arms stretched above the head means pick us up; waving both arms
means need help.

## Phonetic alphabet and saying numbers

For spelling names and places clearly over radio: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta,
Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar,
Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee,
Zulu. Numbers are said digit by digit, with "niner" for 9.

## Radio procedure words (what over, out and roger mean)

Over: I have finished talking, your turn. Out: conversation over (never "over
and out"). Roger: message received. Wilco: will comply. Say again: repeat.
Break: pause or separate parts of a message. Affirmative / negative: yes /
no. Stand by: wait. Listen before transmitting, keep it short, press the
button a second before speaking, and give your name or call sign.
