# First aid (emergency notes)

## First steps at any emergency

Check the scene is safe for you first. Call emergency services if you can
(911 in North America, 112 in Europe and from most mobile phones, 999 UK,
000 Australia). Check the person: responsive? breathing normally? heavy
bleeding? Treat in that order: not breathing, then severe bleeding, then
shock, then everything else. Stay with them and keep them warm.

## CPR (someone is not breathing)

If the person does not respond and is not breathing normally (gasping is not
normal breathing), start CPR. Kneel beside them, put the heel of one hand in
the centre of the chest, the other hand on top. Push hard and fast: 5 to 6 cm
(about 2 inches) deep, 100 to 120 pushes per minute, letting the chest come
all the way back up. If trained, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30
compressions; otherwise hands-only compressions. Keep going until help
arrives, an AED is ready, or the person breathes normally. Use an AED
(defibrillator) as soon as one is available and follow its voice prompts.

## CPR on a child or baby

Child (1 year to puberty): one or two hands, about 5 cm deep (one third of the
chest), 100 to 120 per minute, 30 compressions then 2 breaths. Baby (under 1
year): two fingers in the centre of the chest just below the nipple line,
about 4 cm deep, 100 to 120 per minute, 30 compressions then 2 gentle puffs
covering mouth and nose. For drowning and for children, give breaths if you
can: their arrest is usually from lack of oxygen.

## Choking

If the person can cough or speak, encourage them to keep coughing. If they
cannot breathe, speak or cough: give up to 5 sharp back blows between the
shoulder blades, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts (stand behind, fist just
above the belly button, pull sharply in and up). Repeat until it clears. If
they become unresponsive, start CPR. Pregnant or large person: chest thrusts
instead of abdominal thrusts. Baby: face down along your forearm, 5 back
blows, then turn over and give 5 chest thrusts with two fingers; never
abdominal thrusts on a baby.

## Severe bleeding and tourniquets

Press firmly and directly on the wound with a cloth or dressing, and keep
pressing; add more cloth on top rather than removing soaked cloth. Raise the
limb if possible. For bleeding from an arm or leg that will not stop with
pressure, use a tourniquet: put it 5 to 7 cm (2 to 3 inches) above the wound, not
on a joint, and tighten until the bleeding stops. Write down the time it was
applied and do not loosen it; it hurts, that is normal. Pack deep wounds
(not the chest or belly) tightly with cloth and press.

## Shock

Signs: pale, cold, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confusion,
weakness, thirst. Lay the person down, raise the legs about 30 cm if there is
no leg, hip, head or spine injury, keep them warm with a blanket, treat the
cause (usually bleeding), and give nothing to eat or drink. Reassure them and
watch their breathing.

## Recovery position

For someone unresponsive but breathing normally: roll them on their side,
top leg bent at the knee to stop them rolling, head tilted back so the airway
stays open and fluids can drain from the mouth. Check their breathing
regularly. Start CPR if it stops.

## Burns

To treat a burn, cool it under cool running water for at least 10 minutes, ideally 20.
Remove rings, watches and clothing near the burn unless stuck to it. Cover
loosely with cling film or a clean non-fluffy cloth. No ice, butter, oil,
toothpaste or creams; do not burst blisters. Get help for burns larger than
the person's hand, on the face, hands, feet, groin or joints, chemical or
electrical burns, and any burn on a child. Keep the person warm: cooling a
large burn can cause hypothermia.

## Broken bones, sprains and splints

Keep the injured part still. Splint a suspected fracture in the position you
found it, padding it, and immobilise the joint above and below (sticks,
rolled newspaper, a board, or the other leg; a sling for an arm). Check the
hand or foot stays warm and pink after tying. Do not try to straighten it.
Sprain: rest, ice wrapped in cloth for 20 minutes at a time, compression
bandage, and elevation.

## Head, neck and spine injury

Suspect a spine injury after a fall from height, a car crash, or a diving
accident. Keep the head and neck still, in line with the body; do not move
the person unless they are in danger. After a head blow, watch for: worsening
headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, drowsiness, unequal pupils, fluid from
nose or ears, seizure. Those need urgent care. Someone with a concussion
should not be left alone for the first day.

## Heart attack

Signs: chest pain, pressure or tightness that may spread to the arm, neck,
jaw or back; shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, light-headedness. Women
and older people often have milder or unusual symptoms. Call for help, have
them sit and rest. If they are not allergic to aspirin and have not been told
to avoid it, have them chew one regular aspirin (300 to 325 mg). Be ready to
start CPR.

## Stroke (BE FAST)

Signs of a stroke come on suddenly. Check BE FAST: Balance lost, Eyes (sudden
vision loss), Face drooping on one side, Arm weak (cannot raise both), Speech
slurred or strange, Time: note when it started and get help immediately. Do
not give food, drink or aspirin. If unresponsive and breathing, use the
recovery position.

## Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)

Signs: swelling of face, lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, wheezing,
hives, dizziness, collapse, soon after food, a sting or a medicine. Use their
epinephrine (adrenaline) auto-injector into the outer thigh, through clothing
if needed. If there is no improvement after 5 to 15 minutes and a second
injector is available, give it. Let them sit up if breathing is hard, or lie
down with legs raised if faint. They need medical care even if they improve.

## Seizure

Protect the person from injury: move hard objects away, cushion the head. Do
not hold them down and put nothing in their mouth. Time the seizure. A
seizure longer than 5 minutes, repeated seizures, or one in water or during
pregnancy is an emergency. When it stops, put them in the recovery position
and stay with them; confusion afterwards is normal.

## Low blood sugar (diabetes)

Signs: shaking, sweating, pale, confused, irritable, hungry. If they can
swallow, give 15 grams of fast sugar: half a glass of juice or regular soda,
3 to 4 glucose tablets, or a tablespoon of sugar or honey. Check again after
15 minutes and repeat if still low, then give a snack. If they cannot
swallow safely, give nothing by mouth and get help.

## Hypothermia (too cold)

Signs of hypothermia: shivering, confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness, drowsiness;
shivering may stop when it gets severe. Treatment: move them out of the cold and wind,
remove wet clothing, wrap in dry blankets including the head, and insulate
them from the ground. Warm the core (chest, neck, armpits, groin) first, with
skin-to-skin contact if needed. Give warm sweet drinks only if fully alert. No
alcohol, and do not rub the limbs. Handle a severely cold person gently.

## Frostbite

Signs: numbness, skin white, grey-yellow, waxy or hard. Get out of the cold.
Rewarm only if it will not freeze again: soak in warm (not hot) water, about
37 to 39 °C (body temperature), for 15 to 30 minutes. Do not rub it or use
direct heat such as a fire or heater; the numb skin burns easily. Do not
walk on frostbitten feet if avoidable.

## Heat exhaustion and heat stroke

Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, cold clammy skin, weakness, headache,
nausea, cramps. Move to shade, loosen clothing, cool with wet cloths, give
sips of water. Heat stroke is an emergency: very hot skin (40 °C / 104 °F or
more), confusion, slurred speech, collapse or seizure; sweating may have
stopped. Cool them fast: immerse in cold water if possible, otherwise soak
with water and fan, ice packs to neck, armpits and groin. Give no drinks if
confused.

## Dehydration and oral rehydration solution

Signs: thirst, dark urine or little urine, dry mouth, dizziness, and in
children few wet diapers and no tears. Oral rehydration solution (WHO home
recipe): 1 litre of clean water, 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level
teaspoon of salt, stirred until dissolved. Sip it often, especially with
diarrhoea or vomiting. Keep breastfeeding babies.

## Cuts and wounds: cleaning and signs of infection

Wash your hands, then rinse the wound with plenty of clean (boiled and cooled,
or drinkable) water for several minutes and remove visible dirt. Cover with a
clean dressing and change it daily or when wet. Do not tightly close deep,
dirty or bite wounds. Signs of infection: spreading redness, warmth,
swelling, pus, red streaks from the wound, fever. Those need medical care.
Animal bites carry a rabies risk: get help.

## Nosebleed

Sit up and lean forward (not back). Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for
10 to 15 minutes without letting go, breathing through the mouth. Get help if
it lasts more than 20 minutes or followed a head injury.

## Eye injury and chemicals in the eye

Chemical in the eye: flush with clean running water for at least 15 to 20
minutes, holding the eye open, the injured eye downward so water runs away
from the other eye. Do not rub. Object stuck in the eye: do not remove it;
cover both eyes loosely and get help.

## Poisoning

Call poison control or emergency services if you can (US poison control
1-800-222-1222). Do not make the person vomit. Keep the container to show what
was taken. Chemical on skin: remove contaminated clothing, rinse with lots of
water. Carbon monoxide: get the person into fresh air (see the power and home
safety notes).

## Snake bite

Keep the person still and calm, and get help; carry them if possible. Keep
the bitten limb still, at or around heart level. Remove rings, watches and
tight clothing before swelling. Do not cut the bite, suck it, or apply ice or a
tourniquet. Note the time and the snake's appearance, without trying to catch
it. (In Australia, use a pressure immobilisation bandage instead.)

## Drowning

Do not enter the water unless trained: reach with a pole or throw something
that floats. Once out, if not breathing normally, give 5 rescue breaths if
trained, then start CPR. Everyone rescued from drowning needs medical checks,
even if they seem fine: breathing problems can appear hours later.
